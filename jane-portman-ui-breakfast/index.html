01d · Ranking blog article

Design Tokens, Design Systems, and the Adoption Problem Nobody Talks About

Word count: ~1,150 Target keyword: design tokens in design systems Slug: /design-tokens-design-systems-jan-six

I've been hosting UI Breakfast since 2014, and design systems are one of those topics that keeps coming back — because the field keeps maturing, and because so many teams are still trying to figure out how to make one that people actually use. My conversation with Jan Six, staff product designer at GitHub and co-creator of Tokens Studio, is one of the more practical episodes I've done on it, because Jan doesn't just talk theory. He built the tooling.

If you've heard the phrase "design tokens" thrown around and nodded along without being totally sure what it means in practice, this one's for you. And if you're a solo founder or a small team wondering whether a design system is even worth the effort, stick around — the answer is more nuanced than the design-Twitter hype suggests.

What design tokens actually are

At the simplest level, design tokens are the named, reusable decisions inside your design — your colors, spacing, typography, radii, and so on — stored in a way that both design tools and code can share. Instead of "this button is #3366FF," you have a token like color.primary, and everything that should be primary points to it. Change the token, and everything downstream updates.

That sounds mundane until you see what it unlocks. Jan's whole point is that tokens are the connective tissue between design and development. They're how a decision made in Figma stays consistent when it lands in the codebase — and how you avoid the classic drift where the design file says one thing and production says another.

How components and tokens correlate on a technical level

One of the things I appreciated about this conversation is that Jan went past the marketing version of design systems and into how components and tokens actually correlate on a technical level. A component — a button, a card, an input — is made up of decisions, and each of those decisions can be expressed as a token. When your components consume tokens rather than hard-coded values, you get a system that can flex: theme it, rebrand it, adapt it for density or accessibility, all without rebuilding every component by hand.

This is the part that makes tokens more than a tidy naming convention. Done well, they're the mechanism that lets a design system scale across products and teams without everything turning into a fragile pile of one-off overrides.

Why adoption is the real challenge

Here's the line from the episode I want every team to sit with: the hardest part of a design system isn't building it. It's adoption.

You can create the most elegant token structure in the world, and it means nothing if your designers and developers don't reach for it. Jan is refreshingly honest about this. Adoption is a people problem more than a technical one. It's about making the system easier to use than not using it, about documentation that people can actually find, and about tying the system into the way teams already work instead of asking them to change everything at once.

I see this constantly with the SaaS founders in my audience. They get excited about the artifact — the shiny system — and underinvest in the part that actually determines whether it pays off: getting people to use it consistently. If you take one thing from this episode, let it be that adoption is the metric that matters, not the completeness of your token library.

How to get started (without over-engineering)

The other question Jan and I dug into is where to begin, because "build a design system" is intimidating advice for a small team. His guidance is practical: start small, start with the decisions you're already repeating, and let the system grow from real needs rather than a grand upfront plan.

You don't need to tokenize everything on day one. Pull out the handful of values you use everywhere — your core colors, your spacing scale, your type ramp — turn those into tokens, and get your components consuming them. That alone buys you consistency and a foundation you can extend. Naming matters here, and there's real craft to it; Jan pointed to Nathan Curtis's work on naming tokens, and Brad Frost's writing on themeable systems, both of which are worth your time if you want to go deeper.

The mistake is treating a design system like a big-bang launch. It's a product. It ships iteratively, it earns adoption over time, and it's never really "done."

Why this matters even if you're small

I know a chunk of my audience is solo designers and early-stage SaaS founders who wonder if any of this applies to them. It does — just at the right scale. Even a one-person product benefits from having named, reusable decisions, because it removes a hundred tiny "what color was that again?" moments and frees you to focus on the actual work. Tokens are just a disciplined way of not re-deciding the same things forever.

And as you grow — as you bring on a contractor, a second designer, a developer who wasn't there when you made those decisions — the tokens become the shared language that keeps everyone aligned. Starting small now is what makes scaling later painless.

Takeaways

If you're building or maintaining a design system, here's what I'd carry out of this conversation with Jan. First, tokens are the connective tissue between design and code — invest in them, because they're what makes a system scale. Second, adoption is the real challenge; a beautiful system nobody uses is a failed system. Third, start small and let it grow from real, repeated decisions rather than a grand plan.

Design systems reward the teams that treat them as living products, not one-time deliverables. Jan has spent years building the tooling that makes that possible, and this episode is a genuinely useful map if you're somewhere on that journey.
