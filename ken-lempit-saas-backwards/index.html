01d · Ranking blog article

What AI Taught One Founder About the Future of SaaS

Word count: ~1,150 Target keyword: future of SaaS in the AI era Slug: /future-of-saas-ai-datasaur-ivan-lee

I've spent 30 years helping software companies grow, and I started SaaS Backwards to do one thing: reverse-engineer what's actually working for SaaS leaders, and just as importantly, what quietly stopped working. My conversation with Ivan Lee, founder and CEO of Datasaur, is one of the clearest case studies I've had on the show for what the AI era is doing to the SaaS playbook we all grew up on.

Here's the setup. Datasaur didn't start as an AI-native company chasing the current wave. It started as a data annotation platform — solid, defensible, a real business. Then ChatGPT arrived, and the ground moved. Customer priorities changed almost overnight. Roadmaps that had been carefully planned got paused. And Ivan found himself having to rethink three things at once: the product, the go-to-market motion, and the business model. Not sequentially. All at the same time, under pressure.

That's the part I want other founders to sit with, because it's not a Datasaur story. It's the story a lot of SaaS companies are living through right now and haven't fully admitted to themselves.

When your SaaS go-to-market channels stop working

One of the most useful things Ivan surfaced is really about diagnosis, and it's a theme I come back to constantly on this show: when your numbers turn down, you have to figure out whether it's you or the market. For Datasaur, the old SaaS go-to-market channels simply stopped producing the way they used to. Not because the team got worse at running them. Because the market got crowded with AI noise, and the buyer's journey changed underneath them.

This is the trap I see experienced operators fall into. When a channel that used to work starts underperforming, the instinct is to push harder on the same channel: more spend, more volume, more of the same messaging. But if the underlying dynamics have shifted, working harder on a broken channel just burns budget faster. The move Ivan made was the harder, smarter one — he treated declining performance as a signal that the strategy needed to change, not just the effort.

If your trends are down, that's an external indicator that something has changed, either in what you're doing or in the underlying performance of the category. The founders who thrive in this cycle are the ones who read that signal early instead of explaining it away.

Enterprise AI strategy: out-of-the-box tools are a starting line, not a finish line

There's a comfortable story going around that enterprises can just buy ChatGPT Enterprise or Microsoft Copilot and be done with AI. Ivan's take is more honest, and more useful. Those tools are genuinely good starting points. They lower the barrier, they demystify the technology, and they get a whole organization comfortable with AI in the flow of work.

But they hit a ceiling. For regulated enterprises — the ones where a wrong answer has legal, financial, or compliance consequences — a general-purpose model that can't see your private data, your internal workflows, and your specific processes will only take you so far. The value isn't in access to a model. The value is in a system that's trained on the things that actually make your business your business.

That distinction matters far beyond Datasaur, because it reframes where defensibility lives in the AI era. It's not the model. Everyone has access to models. It's the private data, the workflow integration, and the trust that comes from an AI you can actually put in front of a regulator. That's the moat.

The future of SaaS: from selling access to solving the job to be done

The line from this episode I'll be repeating for a while is this: the future of SaaS may depend less on selling software access and more on solving the customer's actual job to be done.

Think about what the traditional SaaS model really sold. It sold access. You paid a subscription and you got to use the tool. The customer still had to do the work; you just gave them a better place to do it. That model was fantastic for two decades. But when AI can do more of the actual work, "here's access to a tool" starts to feel thin. The customer doesn't want a seat. They want the outcome.

Datasaur's shift from a traditional SaaS model toward end-to-end AI solutions is exactly this move in practice. Instead of selling a platform and leaving the customer to operate it, they moved toward delivering the finished result the customer was actually hiring the software to produce. That's a different business model, a different sales conversation, and frankly a different company. But it's aligned with where the value is going.

What this means for your go-to-market

If you're a SaaS CEO or a GTM leader watching your usual playbook produce less than it used to: first, diagnose before you spend. Don't assume the channel is broken because the team is failing, and don't assume the team is fine because the channel used to work. Separate the two, and look at whether your ICP, your messaging, and your prospect experience still match a market AI has reshaped.

Second, be honest about your defensibility. If your product is a thin layer of access over a capability anyone can now buy off the shelf, that's a strategy problem, not a marketing problem. No amount of clever demand gen fixes positioning that AI has commoditized.

Third, move toward the outcome. The companies that will win the next few years aren't necessarily the ones with the best model. They're the ones who solve the customer's real job to be done, with private data and workflow depth a general-purpose tool can't touch.

Ivan and Datasaur didn't get to choose whether the market changed. Nobody does. What they got to choose was how fast they read the signal and how honestly they responded to it. That's the whole game right now.
