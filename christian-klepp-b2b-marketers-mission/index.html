01d · Ranking blog article

From Data Overload to Decision Velocity: What Modern Market Research Really Looks Like

Word count: ~1,150 Target keyword: market research for B2B marketers Slug: /market-research-decision-velocity-molly-strawn

Modern B2B marketing starts with curiosity. I say that a lot on this show, because I believe the marketers who win are the ones who stay curious about their customers instead of assuming they already have the answers. So when I sat down with Molly Strawn-Carreño — Director of Brand Growth at AYTM and co-host of the Curiosity Current podcast — I wanted to unpack a question a lot of teams are quietly wrestling with: how do you modernize market research so that data overload turns into decision velocity, rather than paralysis?

What she said stuck with me, and I think it will stick with you too.

Market research isn't an extra. It's the underpinning.

The first thing Molly named is a mindset problem I see constantly. Marketers treat research as "some extra thing that we need, and we'll get to it eventually." And eventually never comes, because there's always a deadline, always an event, always someone asking "can you promote this?"

Her reframe is sharp: research "really should be the underpinning of your entire strategy, because you can't say that you're a client-obsessed organization if you don't regularly speak to your clients." That line is worth sitting with. So many B2B teams describe themselves as customer-obsessed. But obsession without conversation is just assumption wearing a nicer word. If you're not regularly asking your customers and prospects what they actually struggle with, you don't have customer-centricity — you have a story you tell yourself.

The A/B-testing trap

Molly was honest about something a lot of teams do to feel like they're doing research without actually doing it. They'll "send two different types of emails and determine which one works better" — and then stop. It's a data point, not a strategy. As she put it, they're "just doing it to check a box."

This is the difference between activity and insight. Running a test tells you which subject line won today. It doesn't tell you why your audience is unengaged, what they're afraid of, or what job they're hiring you to do. Real research — honing individual data points into a decision-backed strategy — is a different discipline, and it's the one that compounds.

"Don't be like Tom"

My favorite part of the conversation was a story Molly borrowed from AYTM's CRO. Back in the early days of the internet, he had a sales manager named Tom. Clients were starting to ask, "how can I reach you by email? What's this World Wide Web thing?" The team kept asking Tom for email addresses and computers. Finally Tom called a meeting, held up a rotary phone and said, "This is a tool." Then he held up a computer and said, "This is a toy. Stop playing with toys. Utilize your tools, and do your job."

You know how that ended. Tom didn't progress, and, in Molly's words, "retired into obscurity."

The reason this matters is that we are living through the exact same moment with AI. The instinct to dismiss the new thing as a toy — to protect the way you've always worked — is comfortable and career-ending. The marketers who treat AI as a serious tool, learn it, and fold it into how they work will pull away from the ones who wait for it to blow over.

"Someone who uses AI will take your job"

This is the line I'll be repeating. Molly is blunt about it: "AI is not going to take your job. Someone who knows how to use AI is going to take your job." Staying stagnant, she says, is the single biggest mistake a marketer can make right now.

But — and this is the part I love — she's equally clear that this is not a story about humans becoming obsolete. "We're not selling to AI robots," she said. "We're selling to people." AI can gather data, brainstorm, and speed up the grunt work, but it can't do your actual job, because your job is to create messages and brands that resonate with human beings. Her advice for making yourself indispensable is to be able to say, honestly: "here's how I'm utilizing AI, so you get more bang for your buck with me — and here's why you still need a human in the process."

Why AI is always looking backwards

The deepest point she made was about the limits of the technology itself. "AI in the way that it's built will always be historical. It will always be looking backwards." It can try to predict trends, the same way marketers do, but it can't account for the fact that "humans are bizarre, and we do off-the-wall things when we're presented with off-the-wall challenges." Her example was perfect: no model would have predicted a global pandemic causing people to hoard toilet paper and refined beans.

That's exactly why market research — real, forward-facing, ask-the-humans research — is not something AI replaces. It's the thing that keeps you connected to the messy, unpredictable reality of the people you serve.

What the best B2B brands actually do

So what separates the brands that get this right? Molly's answer came back to customer-centricity backed by data. The best teams keep asking: what is actually keeping our customer up at night right now? What are they afraid of, especially with everything changing around AI? What's making them unable to switch off from work at the end of the day? "You're not going to know," she said, "unless you ask them."

And you don't have to understand every technical detail of how research gets done. As we joked in the episode, you don't need to know exactly how the sausage is made — you just need to know where the sausage is going, and how to use the tools available to get there.

That's decision velocity. Not more data. Better questions, asked of real people, turned into strategy fast enough to matter.
