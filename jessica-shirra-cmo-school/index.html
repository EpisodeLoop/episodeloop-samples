02 · SEO Blog Article

I Charge Up to $20,000 for a Marketing Strategy. Here's the Build-Out.

Word count: ~1,300 Primary keyword: fractional CMO strategy pricing Meta: Jess Shirra on what a $20K marketing strategy really sells, the mistake that kills execution, and turning strategy projects into retainers.

I charge up to $20,000 for a single marketing strategy. When I say that on stage or in a DM, I usually get one of two reactions. Either "how is that even possible?" or "teach me how to do that." So let me answer both, because the number isn't the interesting part. The interesting part is what the client is actually paying for, and why most fractional CMOs never get close to that number even when their thinking is just as good.

Here's the uncomfortable truth I've learned building my fractional practice and now teaching it inside Fractional CMO School: the strategy itself is rarely the problem. I've reviewed strategies from talented marketers that were sharp, well-researched, and completely dead on arrival. Beautiful decks that went straight into a Google Drive folder and never got executed. And a strategy no one executes isn't worth $20,000. It isn't worth $2,000. It's worth nothing, because it changed nothing.

So the real skill — the thing that justifies the price — is building a strategy that actually gets used. Let me walk you through how I do it.

What a $20,000 strategy is actually selling

When a client pays me $20,000, they are not paying for a document. They're paying for clarity and for the confidence that the plan will move revenue. That reframe matters, because the moment you think you're selling a deliverable, you start competing on how thick the deck is and how many hours you put in. That's a losing game. Somebody will always be cheaper and more willing to over-deliver.

I'm not selling hours. I'm selling the outcome the client is really hiring me for: a plan their team will run, that ties to revenue, that they don't have to babysit. Price follows the outcome, not the effort. Once I understood that, my pricing stopped being a negotiation about my time and started being a reflection of the result.

The mistake that kills most strategies

Here's the number one mistake I see fractional CMOs make, and I made it too early on: they build the strategy for the client instead of with the client, and then they "reveal" it.

It feels impressive to disappear for three weeks and come back with a polished master plan. But when you hand someone a finished strategy they had no hand in shaping, you get polite nods and zero ownership. They didn't build it, so they don't defend it, and the first time it's inconvenient, it gets shelved. You did brilliant work and it still failed, because execution lives and dies on ownership.

The fix isn't to work less. It's to bring the client into the build at the right moments so that by the time the strategy is "done," it already feels like theirs. That single shift is the difference between a strategy that gets executed and one that decorates a shared drive.

Why I never deliver strategy in a live meeting

This is the one that surprises people the most. I never present a strategy in a live meeting. Ever.

Think about what happens in a live reveal. You're talking, they're half-listening while forming reactions, someone fixates on slide four and derails the whole thing, and nobody actually absorbs the plan. You spent weeks on the thinking, and it gets judged on your presentation skills and the room's mood that day.

Instead, I deliver the strategy in a format the client can sit with on their own time — read it, reread it, let it land, and come back with real questions instead of reflexive ones. By the time we do talk, they've actually digested it. The conversation is about execution, not first impressions. It protects the work, and honestly, it protects my time. I'm not performing a strategy. I'm handing over a plan and then aligning on how it gets run.

The alignment call: where a project becomes a retainer

Most fractional CMOs treat a strategy as a one-and-done project. Deliver it, invoice it, move on. That's leaving the most valuable part on the table.

After the client has absorbed the strategy, I run an alignment call. On the surface it's about making sure we're on the same page. But it's really where the relationship shifts from "you did a project for me" to "I need you to help me run this." Because the moment a client sees a real plan tied to revenue, the next question is always the same: who's going to make sure this actually happens?

That's the retainer. Not a hard pitch, not a bolt-on upsell. It's the natural next step when the strategy is good enough that executing it well suddenly matters more than the strategy itself. The alignment call is where a $20,000 project quietly becomes a monthly relationship, because you've made the value of ongoing leadership obvious instead of arguing for it.

What this means for your fractional practice

If you want to charge like a CMO instead of a marketer, here's what I'd take from this.

First, stop selling the deck. Sell the outcome — a plan the team will run that moves revenue. Price the result, not your hours, and you stop competing in a race to the bottom.

Second, build with the client, not for them. Ownership is what gets a strategy executed. If they didn't help shape it, they won't defend it, and your best work will die quietly.

Third, protect the strategy in how you deliver it. Don't let weeks of thinking get judged on a live-meeting first impression. Let it land, then align.

Fourth, treat the alignment call as a doorway, not a wrap-up. That's where a one-time project becomes ongoing leadership — if you've made the value clear.

None of this is about working harder or padding the deliverable. It's about understanding what a client is really buying and building the entire experience around getting it executed. That's what a $20,000 strategy is. And it's a skill you can learn.
