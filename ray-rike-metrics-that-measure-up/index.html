02 · SEO Blog Article

The State of SaaS & AI, By the Numbers

Word count: ~1,300 Primary keyword: state of SaaS and AI spend Meta: Cledara's Brad van Leeuwen on the spend data behind SaaS & AI: new purchases down ~50%, AI at 3.5–4%, and the CFO adoption gap.

I built Benchmarkit on a simple conviction: a metric without a benchmark is just a number with an opinion attached. So when we talk about what AI is doing to SaaS, I don't want the narrative — I want the data. On this episode of Metrics that Measure Up, I got exactly that, because my guest, Brad van Leeuwen, Co-Founder and COO of Cledara, sits on top of one of the better datasets in the industry: actual SaaS purchasing behavior across more than 1,000 customers in over 30 countries.

Here's what the numbers say. And some of them should make every SaaS operator sit up.

New SaaS purchases are down about 50%

Start with the headline. Over the last two years, the number of new SaaS solutions companies are purchasing has fallen by roughly 50%. That's not a rounding error. That's a structural shift in buyer behavior.

And it shows up in the behaviors underneath the number. Buyers are deepening relationships with vendors they already have rather than adding new ones. They're renewing for longer terms in exchange for better pricing. And critically, they're far more discerning about what they keep — which means the churn rate a lot of vendors are seeing isn't a product problem, it's a portfolio-pruning problem. The buyer is doing exactly what a disciplined CFO should do in a tighter environment: consolidate, negotiate, cut.

If you want a single instrument for this, Cledara tracks what Brad calls the SaaS Momentum Index — essentially whether companies are spending more or less on SaaS this year versus last. For the first half of 2024, that index sat around 100. Flat. Most buyers simply were not increasing their SaaS spend. And that lines up cleanly with the softer growth guidance so many public SaaS companies gave the market. The macro story and the micro purchasing data are telling the same story.

One nuance worth holding onto: consolidation is happening for traditional SaaS — companies folding point solutions into larger platforms — except for native-AI products, which are actually skewing back toward point solutions. So the "everything consolidates" narrative isn't universal. AI is running its own playbook.

AI is 3.5–4% of software spend — and the trajectory matters more than the level

Now to the part everyone wants to talk about. How much are companies actually spending on AI?

Today, AI is roughly 3.5–4% of total software spend, and that includes the ChatGPT and OpenAI line items. If you've been reading the headlines, that number is probably lower than you'd guess. AI dominates the conversation far more than it dominates the budget.

But a good analyst never reads a level without reading the slope. Break it down by function and the trajectory is the story. Marketing is allocating about 5% of its software purchases to AI. Sales is at about 1.3%. That sounds small — until you learn that the Sales number was 0.3% in January 2024. That's more than a 4x increase in well under two years. The level is modest; the rate of change is not. When a category quadruples off a small base, you're watching the early part of a curve, not a plateau.

The CFO gap is the most important number in the episode

Here's the finding I keep coming back to, because it's a measurement problem, and measurement problems are my whole world.

Based on a survey of more than 300 CFOs, the C-suite still views AI software as experimentation. No tangible ROI delivered yet. That's the official position at the top of the house.

Meanwhile, in Cledara's usage data, adoption is climbing dramatically — and it's being driven primarily by individual contributors. The people doing the work are adopting AI tools quickly. The people holding the budget don't fully see it, because it's happening bottom-up, tool by tool, seat by seat, often under the CFO's radar.

That gap between what the CFO believes and what the org is actually doing is the real state of SaaS and AI right now. And it's a benchmarking failure as much as anything. If your leadership's mental model of AI adoption is "experimentation, no ROI" while your teams are already dependent on these tools, you are making budget and headcount decisions off a number that's two years out of date.

Adoption is viral — but so is churn

The spend data also captures something the demo-day narrative misses. Across Cledara's base, customers are adopting 30–40 new vendors per month. And once one customer picks up a particular AI-native tool, Brad watches it spread virally across the customer base very quickly.

The catch: churn is happening almost as fast as adoption. AI tools are easy to try and easy to drop. High velocity in, high velocity out. For any of us building or investing in AI-native software, that's the metric that should keep you honest. Logo acquisition means very little when net retention is leaking out the back at nearly the same rate. This is exactly why I push operators to look past top-of-funnel counts and toward retention and expansion — a viral trial curve can hide a broken retention curve for a quarter or two, and then it can't.

Brad's productivity thesis

I asked Brad the question everyone eventually asks: does AI reduce the headcount you need to produce the same output? His answer was more nuanced than the doom takes.

He sees AI mostly raising everyone in a department to the same baseline of capability. The tools level the floor. The interesting — and harder — question becomes how a person differentiates above a baseline that everyone now shares. And on headcount, his bet is that AI won't primarily cut jobs. It'll produce more output from the current staff, which shows up as faster revenue growth or improved growth efficiency rather than a smaller org chart. As someone who lives in efficiency metrics, that's the version I find most credible: the gains land in the numerator, not by shrinking the denominator.

What to actually do with this

If I distill the episode into operator moves: assume your buyers are consolidating — new-logo SaaS demand is down ~50%, so compete on depth and renewal value, not just acquisition. Read AI spend by slope, not level: 3.5–4% overall looks small, but Sales going 0.3% → 1.3% does not, so position for the curve. Close your own CFO gap by measuring real AI adoption bottom-up before you decide it has "no ROI" — the ICs already voted. And watch AI retention, not AI signups, because viral adoption with matching churn is a vanity metric in disguise.

The state of SaaS and AI isn't a vibe. It's a set of numbers, and the numbers are more sober — and more precise — than the narrative. That's exactly why we measure.
