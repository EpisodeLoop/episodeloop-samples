02 · SEO Blog Article

Beyond the Bank Balance: When Your Growing Business Actually Needs a Real CFO

Word count: ~1,300 Primary keyword: when does a business need a CFO Meta: NOW CFO's Brit Summerill on the revenue inflection points where bank-balance thinking breaks, the hidden costs of flying blind, and why accounting kills deals.

I started The B2B Growth Blueprint to dig into the systems and processes behind durable growth — the unglamorous machinery that separates companies that scale from companies that just stay busy. My conversation with Brit Summerill, a Partner at NOW CFO who's spent 14 years rebuilding broken financial systems for companies from startup through $60M and beyond, is one of the clearest examples I've had on the show of a system most founders don't fix until it's too late.

Here's the line from Brit that reframed the whole conversation for me: "Nobody comes to me for accounting, they're coming to me for visibility." That's the heart of it. This episode isn't really about bookkeeping. It's about whether you can actually see your business clearly enough to make good decisions — and whether the story your numbers tell will hold up the day someone with a checkbook comes to look.

Most founders run the whole company on one number

In the early days, you manage by bank balance. How much is in the account? Are we okay this month? It works, and honestly it's the right instinct when you're small and every dollar is visible.

But as revenue climbs, that cash-basis, gut-driven approach quietly stacks up risk. You don't actually know your true margins. You have no internal controls. And your books — the ones that felt fine when it was just you — won't survive diligence. Brit's description of that state stuck with me: "They're really just bootstrapping and flying by the seat of their pants, and there's duct tape on the wheels."

The problem is that nothing forces the issue until it's expensive. The business feels fine right up until a funding round, an M&A conversation, or an out-of-nowhere private-equity call shows up. And then the cleanup required can derail the entire deal.

The inflection points where bank-balance thinking breaks

One of the most useful parts of this conversation is that Brit put actual numbers on when to change your system — which is exactly the kind of specificity founders rarely get.

Around $5M in revenue, cash-basis bookkeeping and bank-balance management stop working. That's the point to move to accrual accounting, add revenue recognition, and start tracking basic KPIs so you can see what's actually happening beneath the top line.

Around $10M, you need controllers and real internal controls — not because you've done anything wrong, but because the surface area for error and risk has grown past what one person watching the bank account can manage.

And here's the trap: the founders who wait until $20M to make these shifts create expensive cleanup and avoidable risk. They don't save money by delaying. They defer a smaller bill into a much bigger one, and they carry unnecessary risk the whole way there.

The hidden costs of flying blind

The thing I want other founders to sit with is that the cost of poor visibility isn't just stress. It's concrete, and it's larger than most people think.

Without visibility into true margins, companies waste resources building against their weakest products — pouring energy into things that look busy but don't actually pay. They get denied credit lines and end up resorting to expensive hard-money loans. They overpay taxes and penalties on multi-state activity they didn't track properly. And when controls are missing, they expose themselves to internal theft.

Brit had a phrase for the warning sign: "growing broke." You're busier than ever, revenue is climbing, and yet there's less and less cash. That feeling — working harder for a shrinking bank balance — is the signal that you're flying blind. It's not a cash-flow hiccup. It's a visibility problem wearing a cash-flow costume.

Why accounting kills deals "every time"

This is the part every founder thinking about an eventual exit needs to hear. Brit was blunt: "There's a few things that'll kill a deal. One of them's accounting. Every time."

The numbers around this are sobering. Roughly 50% of owners are forced to sell when they're unprepared — not on their terms, not at their timing. And around 70% of small-business M&A deals fall through. The two biggest deal-killers he sees are messy books that don't tell a clean story, and founder dependency with no succession plan.

The line that I'll be repeating: "There's no bigger way to lose a deal than to walk in the room not knowing what your company's really worth, and the numbers don't tell the story that's in your head." You can have a great business in your gut and still lose the deal because the financials can't back up the story you're telling.

What to actually do about it

The good news is that the fixes are the same whether or not you ever plan to sell — because they're really about running a clearer, more durable business.

Treat your books as if you could be audited tomorrow. That single standard forces the discipline that makes everything else easier. Automate your manual processes so visibility doesn't depend on someone remembering to update a spreadsheet. Benchmark your margins against your industry so you know whether you're actually healthy or just surviving. And build a team that can run the business without you — because founder dependency is both a daily bottleneck and a deal-killer.

This maps almost perfectly onto what I talk about constantly with the founders I work with. The most valuable businesses aren't necessarily the biggest. They're the ones with clean books, strong margins, and systems that don't depend on the founder grinding it out. That's what makes revenue durable — and durable revenue is what a buyer, a lender, or frankly your own future self will pay a premium for.

Brit's whole message is that you should invest in visibility before you need it — accrual accounting, real controls, benchmarked KPIs, and a leadership team that lets you step out. Whether your goal is a credit line, an acquisition, or simply sleeping better at night, doing the foundational work early is what lets you seize the best opportunities and survive the worst, instead of watching a deal fall apart at the table.
