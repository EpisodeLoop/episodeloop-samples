01d · Ranking blog article

I Asked Random Londoners About SEO, LinkedIn & ChatGPT — Here's the Gut-Check for B2B

Word count: ~1,150 Target keyword: how to get recommended by ChatGPT Slug: /chatgpt-vs-google-ai-search-b2b-street-interviews

I run Breaking B2B, and most of what I do all day is help B2B and SaaS companies drive pipeline from organic search. So I spend a lot of time inside the marketing bubble — arguing about keywords, LinkedIn algorithms, AI search, GEO, all of it. And every so often it's worth stepping outside that bubble to check whether any of it lands with actual humans. So I took a mic to the streets of central London and asked random members of the public what they think about SEO, LinkedIn, and AI recommendations. No marketers. No founders. Just people.

It was humbling in the best way. Here's what stuck with me, and what it means if you're trying to grow a B2B company right now.

What people outside marketing think LinkedIn is for

We treat LinkedIn like the center of the B2B universe. Ask a marketer and they'll tell you it's the professional network, the demand-gen engine, the founder-brand flywheel. Ask a random Londoner and you get something very different — for a lot of people it's just another social channel they half-check, or a place they go when they're job hunting and then ignore.

That gap matters more than it looks. If the people you're trying to reach don't see LinkedIn the way you do, the way you show up there has to change. It's either a positioning opportunity — you can own a lane most people aren't even paying attention to — or it's a content strategy mistake waiting to happen, if you're posting into a room that isn't listening the way you assume. The point isn't "LinkedIn is dead." The point is: don't confuse how marketers use a channel with how your buyers actually use it.

ChatGPT vs Google: who does the public actually trust?

This was the one I really wanted to test. In our world, everyone has an opinion about AI search — whether ChatGPT is eating Google, whether SEO is dead, all the usual noise. So I asked people directly: when you've got a real decision to make, where do you go, and who do you trust more?

The split was more interesting than a clean winner. People still reach for Google out of habit for a lot of things, but a growing number reach for ChatGPT when they want an answer rather than a list of links — especially when they want something explained or recommended. And that's the part B2B marketers need to sit with. Your buyers are increasingly asking an AI for a recommendation instead of scrolling ten blue links. If you're not the answer the AI gives, you're invisible in that moment, no matter how well you rank on page one of Google.

Why prompts beat keywords now

Here's the mental shift I keep coming back to. For twenty years, the game was keywords: pick a term, rank a page, capture the click. But people don't prompt an AI the way they type a keyword. They ask a full question, with context, in their own words. "What's a good tool for X if I'm a small B2B team and I care about Y?"

You can't rank for a single keyword and expect to win that. What wins is consistent brand signals in the right context — showing up, credibly and repeatedly, everywhere the model learns about your category. Prompts are messier and more specific than keywords, which means the way you get found has to be broader and deeper than one optimized page.

How to actually get recommended by ChatGPT

So how do you become the answer? From everything I'm seeing, it comes down to three things. First, topical depth. Don't publish one thin page on your category — build genuine depth so you're the obvious, comprehensive source on the specific problem you solve. Models reward the source that clearly knows the most.

Second, authoritative mentions. It's not just what's on your site; it's whether other credible places in your category talk about you. Being referenced across the web where the model reads is closer to how this works than the old backlink game — it's about being part of the conversation, not just having a page.

Third, be the obvious answer to a specific problem. Vague, everything-for-everyone positioning is death in AI search. The narrower and clearer you are about the exact problem you solve and who you solve it for, the more likely you are to be the thing the model surfaces when someone prompts that problem.

The lead-gen strategies still working in 2026

None of this means the fundamentals are dead. Quite the opposite. The B2B companies driving predictable pipeline in 2026 are the ones layering old-school SEO fundamentals with GEO — generative engine optimization, getting recommended by AI. SEO still gets you the intent-rich searches on Google. GEO gets you into the AI answers where more and more buyers now start. You don't pick one. You layer them, and you point everything at the specific, high-intent problems your buyers actually search and prompt.

That's the whole game, and it's why I still believe organic search is the best long-term pipeline channel for B2B and SaaS — as long as you stop measuring vanity traffic and start measuring whether you're the answer when it counts.

The street interviews reminded me of something easy to forget in the bubble: your buyers aren't thinking about your marketing anywhere near as much as you are. They ask a question, they trust an answer, they move on. Your job is to be that answer — on Google, in ChatGPT, and everywhere in between.
