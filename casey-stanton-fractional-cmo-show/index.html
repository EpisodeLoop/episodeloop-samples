02 · SEO Blog Article

Ask What Won't Change: The Only Defensible Position Left in Marketing

Word count: ~1,100 Primary keyword: future of marketing agencies Meta: Why SEO agencies, PPC shops, and copywriters are collapsing — and the strategist role that only gets more valuable as AI fractures the marketing industry.

The marketing industry is fracturing fast — and the break isn't happening where most people are looking.

For years, we assumed the disruption would come from the top: big AI platforms swallowing the strategy work, agencies fighting over the scraps of a shrinking pie. But that's not where the damage is showing up. The bottom is getting cut out from under SEO agencies, PPC shops, funnel builders, and copywriters. Not slowly. All at once.

And if you're running a specialty shop, the question isn't whether you can see it. It's whether you can survive it.

The collapse is coming from below

Two forces are hitting at the same time. First, Google's AI overhaul is rewriting the search landscape so thoroughly that SEO agencies are being forced to reinvent themselves or disappear. The playbook that paid for a decade of retainers — rank for keywords, optimize for snippets — is losing its leverage in front of our eyes.

Second, Meta's ad platform is quietly cutting PPC agencies out of their own job. The platform keeps automating the buying decisions that used to require a specialist's judgment. What took an agency and a reporting deck now takes a prompt and a budget. The middle is being removed.

Copywriters and funnel builders are next on the same track. Anyone whose value was "I can operate this tool better than you" is in the crosshairs, because a digital native with a Claude account is already competing directly against them — and the gap keeps closing.

The bottom 80% problem

Here's the uncomfortable part: most of the industry is the bottom 80%.

The work that most agencies and freelancers actually do — the execution, the optimization, the reporting — is exactly the work that AI and hungry newcomers can now do. The 80% who built their practice on being competent operators are the ones feeling the floor drop out. It's not a skill problem. It's a positioning problem.

And the scary part about positioning: you can't fix it by getting better at the thing you used to sell. You have to stop being the person who sells that thing at all.

The only defensible position left

The role that only gets more valuable as the chaos deepens is the strategist and leader. The person who owns the question "what should we do, and why?" — not the person who executes the answer.

That's why the line between a CMO and a Fractional CMO matters so much right now. Strategically, the job is the same: own the marketing outcome for the business. The difference is how you show up — as the person who integrates with leadership, sets direction, and is accountable for results, rather than the person who runs the tactics.

Clients don't seek out the person who can run Facebook ads. They seek out the person who can tell them which bets are worth taking — and who can stand behind the outcome. When everything around you is being commoditized, judgment becomes the product.

How to become the person clients seek out

The shift is simpler to describe than to do:

  • Move up the stack. Stop selling execution. Start selling the decision framework that determines which execution matters.
  • Own outcomes, not deliverables. The person who owns the outcome can't be replaced by a tool. The person who owns the deliverable can.
  • Ask what won't change. This is the question that cuts through the noise. AI is going to change how everything gets made. But what clients will always need — clarity, direction, accountability, the confidence that someone senior is steering — that doesn't change. Position yourself on the side of what won't change, and you're positioning yourself out of the blast radius.

The difference between a CMO and a Fractional CMO

If you're a fractional CMO reading this, you already know: the title isn't the point. The point is that businesses that can't afford — or don't need — a full-time executive still need executive-level marketing leadership. The role is the same. The delivery model is different.

And in a fracturing industry, that's exactly why the role is the only defensible position left. You're not selling a skill. You're selling the thing that skills get pointed at.

The full argument — including where the collapse is hitting first, and how the bottom 80% can reposition before it's too late — is in this week's episode of The Fractional CMO Show.
