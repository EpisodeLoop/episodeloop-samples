01d · Ranking blog article

What Does a Fractional CMO Actually Do?

Word count: ~1,150 Target keyword: what does a fractional CMO do Slug: /what-does-a-fractional-cmo-actually-do

It's the question I get asked more than any other, so I put it at the centre of this episode of Fractional Futures with Rachael Wheatley, a fellow Chartered Fractional CMO. The honest answer is that "fractional CMO" has become a fashionable label, and fashionable labels get fuzzy fast. So Rachael and I set out to be precise about what the role actually delivers — and, just as importantly, what it isn't.

Here's the shortest version I can give you. A fractional CMO is senior marketing leadership, brought in part-time, to make sure the strategic decisions get made and owned by someone who actually knows how. Not more hands to run more campaigns. A head to decide which campaigns are worth running in the first place.

Why businesses reach for tactics when they need strategy

The pattern I see again and again is this. Growth softens, the numbers wobble, and the instinct is to do more marketing. Launch another campaign. Post more. Try a new channel. Spend more. It feels like action, and action feels like progress.

But more activity is not a strategy. When marketing accountability sits with a junior team, or with a well-intentioned leader who doesn't have formal marketing expertise, the business defaults to tactics because tactics are visible and strategy is hard. The result is a lot of motion and not much movement. As Rachael put it, the job isn't doing more stuff — it's doing the right stuff.

That distinction sounds simple. It is not. Deciding what not to do, where to focus a limited budget, and which marketing task actually matters at this stage of the business — that's strategic work, and it's exactly the work that goes undone when nobody senior owns it.

Building marketing capability at three levels

One of the ideas I most wanted to get across in this conversation is that a good fractional CMO doesn't just do the marketing. They build capability so the organisation gets stronger, at three levels.

The first level is strategy: setting a clear direction, aligned to commercial goals, so every pound spent has a job to do. The second is the team and its skills: mentoring the people you already have, so their day-to-day activity is pointed at the right targets rather than kept busy. The third is the wider organisation: helping the whole business — not just the marketing function — understand its customers and act with them in mind.

This is the part that separates embedded leadership from an agency retainer. An agency does the work and takes the capability with them when they leave. A fractional CMO does the work and leaves the capability behind. When the engagement ends, the business is genuinely more able than when you started. That's the point.

Reframing marketing as an investment

If there's one mindset shift I'd want every CEO and founder to take from this episode, it's this: stop treating marketing as a cost to be minimised and start treating it as an investment to be optimised.

A cost is something you cut when times are tight. An investment is something you manage for a return. The moment marketing becomes an investment in the conversation, everything changes — you start asking what return each activity produces, you stop tolerating activity that can't be tied to an outcome, and you make decisions with the discipline you'd apply to any other use of capital. That framing is a large part of what a fractional CMO brings into the boardroom.

Culture, customer centricity, and journey mapping

Strategy on paper is worthless if the organisation doesn't live it, which is why Rachael and I spent real time on culture. Aligning organisational culture with customer centricity means the whole business — sales, product, service, leadership — makes decisions with the customer's actual experience in mind, not just the marketing team.

The practical tool we kept coming back to is customer journey mapping. When you map the real journey your buyers take — from the first time they become aware of you, through evaluation, to becoming and staying a customer — you stop guessing and start seeing. You see where the experience is strong and, more usefully, where it leaks. I've long been a believer in the "leaky bucket" idea: you can pour endless leads into the top, but if the journey leaks at the touchpoints that matter, you never fill the bucket. Journey mapping is how you find the leaks.

When do you actually need one?

I won't pretend every business needs a fractional CMO. You need one when the strategic marketing decisions are being made by people who aren't equipped to make them — or aren't being made at all. When marketing accountability has quietly landed on a junior team, or on a founder already stretched thin, and the business is defaulting to tactics because there's no senior head to set direction. That's the signal. Not "we should do more marketing," but "nobody senior actually owns this."

If that sounds like your business, the fix isn't more activity. It's leadership — the right stuff, done deliberately, with someone accountable for the commercial outcome.
