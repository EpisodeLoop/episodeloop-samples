02 · SEO Blog Article

Brand Voice Is Not a Feeling: How to Define Yours in 3 Measurable Parts

Word count: ~1,100 Primary keyword: how to define your brand voice Meta: Brand voice isn't "friendly, human, casual" — it's vocabulary, tone, and cadence. Justin Blackman's framework for defining and scaling a brand voice.

Ask most founders to describe their brand voice and you'll get the same four words: friendly, human, casual.

Ask them what those words mean — what their brand would actually say, or never say, in a given situation — and the answer evaporates. "Friendly" describes a vibe, not a voice. It's a feeling, and you can't brief a writer with a feeling.

Justin Blackman calls this the Trifecta of Nothingness, and on episode #048 of The Message-Market Fit Podcast, he offers the antidote: a brand voice you can actually measure.

Voice is a system, not a vibe

Justin's path to this framework is as unconventional as the idea itself. He spent years doing face-to-face consumer sampling for Red Bull and Five Hour Energy, learning to read rooms in real-time — if your pitch doesn't land in three seconds on a street corner, you find out immediately. He built his portfolio by writing 100 headlines a day for 100 days.

That's the background that produced the core insight of this episode: brand voice breaks down into three measurable components.

  • Vocabulary — the words you use, and just as importantly, the words you refuse to use.
  • Tone — the attitude you take toward the reader, the emotional register of the sentence.
  • Cadence — the rhythm: sentence length, punctuation, the beat of the copy.

When you can describe your voice in those three terms, you can brief it, test it, and scale it. When you can't, you're stuck with "friendly, human, casual" — and hoping everyone interprets it the same way. They won't.

The Trifecta of Nothingness

Here's why "friendly, human, casual" is worse than useless: it's undefinable, untestable, and unenforceable. Three different writers will produce three different voices from the same three adjectives, and none of them will be wrong — which means none of them is right.

What actually distinguishes brands is what they say and how they say it, specifically. A brand voice that sounds like the brand — not like a generic friendly company — is what Justin calls Brand Ventriloquism: throwing your voice so it sounds exactly like the brand, consistently, whoever's writing.

Why voice of customer belongs in editing, not writing

One of the sharpest points in the conversation: voice of customer (VOC) is an editing tool, not a writing engine.

If you write directly from customer language, you get a transcript, not a voice. But when you use VOC as the lens you edit through — checking whether the copy sounds like the person you're writing for would actually hear themselves in it — it becomes powerful.

The distinction matters more as AI tools get better at generating copy on demand. The people who can catch whether the output is on-voice are the ones who own the process. The people who can't are the ones who get "doesn't sound right" feedback forever.

Scaling voice across a team — and across AI

The episode also covers the practical question: once you've defined the voice, how do you keep it consistent as you scale?

Voice guidelines, done properly, are more than a mood board — for personal brands, Justin's guides run up to 100 pages, covering vocabulary, tone, cadence, examples, and anti-examples. The goal isn't a rulebook for its own sake; it's that every writer, every contractor, every AI prompt gets pointed at the same target.

And crucially: the voice needs room to evolve without losing what makes it distinct. A voice that can't change is a voice that's already outdated. The tension isn't between consistency and growth — it's between a defined system and an undefined vibe. One evolves deliberately. The other just drifts.

What to do this week

If you're a founder or marketing leader who's been told to "just be yourself" — or a copywriter drowning in "it doesn't sound right" feedback — start here:

  • Write down the words your brand uses, and the words it never would. That's vocabulary.
  • Describe the attitude your brand takes toward the reader — not your company's personality, but how the copy feels. That's tone.
  • Look at your best-performing piece. Now look at its rhythm: sentence lengths, punctuation, pacing. That's cadence.

Three columns on one page beats four adjectives every time.
